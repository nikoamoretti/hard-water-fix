## What that film is

USGS: hardness is dissolved calcium and magnesium. Hose water evaporates on the spout nose, hose threads, and vacuum-breaker cap. The Ca and Mg stay as white or tan crust. Orange on a well bib is iron rust, not the same job as scale. That split is the rest of this page.

If you want the hardness number, [test the tap](/articles/how-to-test-water-hardness-at-home).

## Name the bib. Then name the stain.

Chrome or brass-body metal you can rinse: vinegar bag first. CLR only if crust remains, two minutes. Painted sleeve or frost-free plastic body: vinegar rag only. No CLR on paint or plastic. Matte-black indoor faucets are not this page.

White or tan crust is scale. Orange or brown on the metal bib from well water is rust. Those are different jobs. Black slime in the hose threads is mold or hose gunk. Bleach can be a mold path. Bleach does nothing to calcium. Do not bleach a mineral job.

## Shut the interior valve. Pull the hose. Screen first.

Do not bag acid on a live hose still spraying the lawn. Many houses have a shutoff on the interior side of the sillcock. Close it if you have one. Open the outdoor handle briefly to drop pressure, then close it again.

1. Unscrew the garden hose. Drop a towel under the bib so small parts do not bounce into the mulch.
2. Look at the **hose-thread screen** — a small washer-screen that sits in the male hose threads or the female hose end. This is not a [kitchen aerator](/articles/how-to-clean-a-clogged-faucet-aerator). Pull it. Set rubber washers aside. They do not soak overnight in acid.
3. Wash mud and algae off with mild dish soap first. Dirt shields mineral.
4. If anything nearby still smells like bleach (deck wash, mold spray), rinse until it doesn't. Then start. Never mix leftover bleach with vinegar, CLR, or Iron Out.

Poison Control: mixing bleach with an acid, including vinegar, forms chlorine gas. Washington State DOH names vinegar as one of those acids, and rust-removal products too. Leave the area and breathe fresh air if you already mixed them. Poison Help **1-800-222-1222**.

## Vinegar bag or rag on the spout and threads. Not overnight on rubber.

**Hose off. Screen out. Washers out.** Distilled white vinegar. Grocery bottle. Pantry. No Amazon vinegar.

Pantry acid vs the bottle is [vinegar vs CLR](/articles/vinegar-vs-clr-for-hard-water-stains). Light film: vinegar. Raised crust that a fingernail catches on chrome or brass-body: CLR after this bag. Do not retread that comparison here.

1. Fill a zip bag with distilled white vinegar, or soak a rag. Full-strength for the bag. No baking-soda fizz.
2. Pull the bag over the spout nose and hose threads. Rubber band or zip tie. Rag wrap works the same on the vacuum-breaker cap and the packing-nut area — do not force a seized handle.
3. Sit **20–30 minutes**. Not overnight. Not on a freeze night. Rubber washers stay out of the bag.
4. Soft brush or old toothbrush on the threads, spout nose, and screen mesh. No steel wool. No scouring powder on chrome.
5. Dump the bag away from plants you care about. Do not fizz this soak with baking soda. That bubble is CO2 leaving, not extra scale power.

Painted sleeves and frost-free plastic stay on this vinegar rag. No citric on this page. No Granite Gold. No Lime-A-Way.

## Distilled last rinse. Tap water puts the mineral back.

Hard hose water is how the crust got on the threads. After vinegar (and after CLR, if you use it), rinse the spout, threads, and screen with grocery distilled water (1 gal). Then towel dry.

A tap rinse on very hard water can drop a fresh film on the same threads you just cleaned. Distilled has no calcium to redeposit. Then dry. Leave it wet and the spots come back the same afternoon.
