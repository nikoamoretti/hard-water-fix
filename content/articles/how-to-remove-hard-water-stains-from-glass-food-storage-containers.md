## Food vs mineral honesty: soap first for grease or tomato film

Do the food-film check before you open the vinegar. Look at the stain in daylight. Rub a small patch with warm water and a drop of mild dish soap.

- White crust that feels chalky and does not smear orange or greasy is **mineral film**. Distilled white vinegar **1:1 soak** is the job. Soft non-abrasive sponge. Distilled pantry rinse. **Hand-dry.**
- Orange tomato smear, oily leftover film, or a greasy ring that soap lifts is **food film**, not calcium. Honesty: **grease or tomato film ≠ white mineral.** Soap first. Rinse. Then, if white chalk is still on the glass, do the vinegar mineral pass.
- Both can live in the same leftover box. Vinegar will not degrease tomato sauce. Soap will not dissolve chalk. Treat them in that order: **soap first**, vinegar second.

A mineral soak on top of thick sauce wastes the acid and leaves pickle smell in the grease. Empty, soap, rinse, then soak.

## Etching honesty: permanent haze after acid is etching

Do the wet test on a dry, uncleaned patch, in daylight. Run a fingernail across the haze. Dampen that same patch. Look again.

- White crust that feels chalky and fades while wet is **mineral film**. Distilled white vinegar **1:1 soak** is still the job. Soft non-abrasive sponge. Distilled pantry rinse. Hand-dry.
- Smooth frost that looks **identical wet and dry** after the acid pass is **etch** — the glass surface itself is pitted. Household acid will not un-etch leftover glass. Honesty: **permanent haze after acid is etching.** Stop soaking. Do not reach for CLR “to get the cloud out.” Replace the box if the frost stays after chalk is gone. Do not invent a replacement ASIN.
- Both can live on the same leftover box. Vinegar will clear the coat and leave the frost. Believe the frost.
