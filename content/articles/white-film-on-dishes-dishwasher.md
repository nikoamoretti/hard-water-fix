A dishwasher that “has always been fine” can start leaving a white film after a detergent change, a move to a harder water supply, or a rinse-aid dispenser that ran dry. The film is usually one of two things, and they look identical until you test.

1. **Hard-water mineral film.** Calcium and magnesium dry onto the glass when the final rinse beads instead of sheeting off.
2. **Etching.** The glass surface itself is microscopically eaten away. That damage is permanent.

There is a third lookalike: leftover detergent or food protein film. That one often feels slick or soapy rather than chalky, and it shows up when the water never got hot enough or you used far too much detergent.

## Do the vinegar test first

This is the test appliance makers have been publishing for years, including GE and Whirlpool.

Take one filmed glass. Soak a corner in distilled white vinegar for a few minutes, or rub that corner with a vinegar-damp cloth. Rinse and dry.

- **The white comes off.** You have mineral film (or a mix of minerals and removable residue). Keep reading. You can fix the dishes you already have and change how you run the machine.
- **The white does not move, and the glass looks dull even when dry.** That is likely etching. More acid will not restore the shine. Hand-wash the pieces you care about and change the wash chemistry so the rest of the set does not follow.

Do this test before you buy a new dishwasher. A new machine on the same hard water, with an empty rinse-aid tank, will print the same film.

## Why the film showed up after 2010

If you have been washing dishes the same way for fifteen years and the film is new, the detergent changed under you. Around 2010, phosphates were removed from household dishwasher detergents in the United States. Phosphates had been very good at tying up hardness minerals. Without them, the same water starts leaving a chalky layer on glass.

GE’s detergent notes say a citric-acid wash cycle strips that film, and a rinse agent helps keep it from coming back. University of Florida’s consumer guidance puts a number on prevention: if water tests at about **6 grains per gallon (gpg)** or harder, you may need softened water to fully stop hardness filming. Below that, the right detergent dose plus a rinse agent is often enough.

## Clear the film you already have

Work on dishes that are already clean aside from the haze. Food soil will confuse the result.

### Citric acid cycle (best first reset)

1. Scrape, but do not add detergent.
2. Take out silverware, aluminum, and other metals. Acid plus metal is how you get pitting and discoloration.
3. Put filmed glassware and dishes in the racks as usual.
4. Add citric acid in the **main detergent cup** only. GE sells a kit; grocery food-grade citric acid works. Use the amount the kit lists, or about the volume of a normal detergent fill if you are using crystals.
5. Run a complete hot cycle.
6. Inspect a glass dry, in daylight.

If the film is lighter but not gone, run the same cycle once more. Do not add regular detergent on top of the acid.

### Vinegar rinse (lighter film, or no citric acid in the house)

GE’s version: pour **1 cup of white vinegar** into the dishwasher just after the detergent cup opens, then let the cycle finish. Other manufacturers describe a bowl of vinegar on the top rack of an otherwise empty hot cycle to descale the tub.

Two cautions:

- Do not fill the rinse-aid dispenser with vinegar as a standing practice. The dispenser and its seals are built for rinse aid. Vinegar is a different chemistry and a different viscosity.
- Repeat a few times a year if needed, not every night.

After either reset, wash a normal load with detergent **and** rinse aid and see whether fresh film appears. If the old film is gone and new loads come out clear, you are done with the archaeology. The rest is prevention.

## Make the next load stay clear

### Fill the rinse aid and turn the dose up

Rinse aid is not optional garnish in hard water. It cuts the surface tension of the final rinse so water sheets off instead of drying in droplets. Those droplets are how minerals become spots.

Most machines have a reservoir next to the detergent door and a dose setting, often 1–6. Factory default is frequently too low for hard water. If the reservoir is empty, you will get spotting even with an expensive detergent pod.

Not every pod contains a rinse agent. Read the box. If it does not, you still need the dispenser.

### Match detergent to the water, not to the size of the cup

In **hard** water, under-dosing detergent leaves minerals free to plate out. In **soft** or softened water, over-dosing detergent is one of the ways glass gets etched. Whirlpool’s product help is blunt about this: detergents are designed to expect some hardness. When the water is already very soft and the detergent is strong and heavy, that extra chemistry has nothing to bind but the glass.

Practical rules:

- Use a quality tablet or pack in the closed main cup. Powder and gel are easier to overdose.
- Do not add a second pod “for extra dirty pans.”
- If you have a whole-house softener, start with less detergent than the box maximum and keep rinse aid on a lower setting.
- If you are on a hard well, keep rinse aid high and do not starve the detergent cup.

### Heat, loading, and the hardware that actually rinses

Film also shows up when the machine never rinses what it washed.

- Run the hottest normal cycle you have until the film is under control. Cold short cycles leave detergent and minerals behind.
- Do not block the detergent door with a pan.
- Do not nest bowls so water cannot reach the inner surfaces.
- Pull the filter and rinse it. A packed filter recirculates grit.
- Check that both spray arms spin and that food is not plugging the jets. A toothpick clears most holes.
- If glasses on the top rack film and plates on the bottom do not, look at the upper arm and how you loaded the top rack.

If the rinse-aid level never drops after a week of daily loads, the dispenser may be stuck. That is a parts problem, not a “buy a new detergent brand” problem.

## When the glass is etched

Etching looks like a permanent frost. It is often worse on the parts of a glass that faced the spray. A fingernail may catch a very slight roughness, or the surface may still feel smooth and just look tired.

Common contributors, from manufacturer help pages:

- Very soft water plus a lot of aggressive detergent
- High heat plus that same overdose
- Washing already-etched glass over and over, which makes the dullness more obvious

There is no dishwasher cycle that replates glass. Retire those pieces as stemware and change the detergent dose so replacements last.

## A short plan for this weekend

1. Vinegar-wipe one glass. Decide film vs etch.
2. If film: citric-acid cycle, metals out, no detergent.
3. Fill rinse aid. Raise the dose if your water is hard.
4. Clean the filter and spin the spray arms by hand.
5. Run one normal hot load with a single detergent pack.
6. If glasses still film and your water tests above about 6 gpg, look at softening. The dishwasher is doing what hard water tells it to do.

That is the whole problem for most kitchens. It is not a mysterious “bad batch of pods.” It is minerals, rinse chemistry, and the occasional ruined glass that no cleaner will resurrect.
