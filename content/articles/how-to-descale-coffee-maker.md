A coffee maker slows down, brews cooler, and eventually coughs white flakes into the carafe because hard water leaves calcium carbonate on the heating path. That crust is limescale. Descaling is an acid rinse that dissolves it.

The acid you should use is the one in the booklet that came with the machine, not the one that is cheapest on the pantry shelf. Manufacturers disagree, and they disagree in writing. Using the wrong acid can leave a sour taste, chew on aluminum or copper, or give the company a reason to reject a warranty claim.

## How to tell the machine needs a descale

You do not need a water-hardness lab report to see the usual signs:

- Brew time stretches. A pot that used to finish in four minutes now crawls.
- Coffee comes out lukewarm.
- You hear more rumbling or sputtering than you used to.
- White or tan flakes show up in the first cup.
- A “descale” or “clean” light is on. That light is not a suggestion. It is a flow or count trigger.

Hard water shortens the interval. If you brew every day on well water or a hard municipal supply, a drip machine may want a descale every 1–3 months. Softer water can go longer. A tank filter or a pitcher filter stretches the interval. It does not replace descaling.

## Read the manual for 90 seconds

Look for the words **descale**, **decalcify**, or **clean cycle**. Write down three things:

1. Which product they name (vinegar mix, citric acid, or a branded bottle).
2. Whether they say **do not use vinegar**.
3. How many plain-water rinse cycles they want at the end.

Then follow that, even if a generic internet chart says something else. The chart below is only for when the booklet is silent or lost.

## Which acid for which machine

**Branded descaling solution.** This is the default for espresso machines, super-automatics, and most pod systems. Formulas are usually lactic, sulfamic, or citric acids balanced for rubber gaskets and metal boilers. Nespresso tells owners to use its descaling kit and says other solutions, including vinegar, may damage the machine. Jura tells owners to use JURA tablets and warns that vinegar-type agents can damage the machine or leave residue. De’Longhi’s line is “use the De’Longhi descaler and never vinegar,” citing smell, taste, and the risk of faults that the guarantee will not cover.

**Food-grade citric acid.** A common third-party option when the maker does not forbid it. It chelates calcium well and does not leave the same lingering odor as vinegar. A widely used starting mix is **1–2 tablespoons (roughly 15–20 g) per liter of water**, a bit stronger in very hard water, weaker if you know the boiler is aluminum. If the warranty booklet names only the branded packet, use the packet until the warranty ends.

**Distilled white vinegar.** Cheap and effective on scale in simple drip machines. Cuisinart’s PerfecTemp DCC-3200 booklet, for example, says to fill the reservoir with **1/3 white vinegar and 2/3 water** and run the CLEAN cycle. Other drip booklets use 1:1 vinegar and water. Vinegar is acetic acid. It clings to plastic tanks and silicone tubes. If you can still smell it after two rinse tanks, run a third. Cleaning vinegar (a higher acid percentage) is the wrong bottle.

**Machines that tell you not to use vinegar — believe them.** Besides Nespresso, Jura, and De’Longhi, some copper-element brewers ban both vinegar and citric acid. Moccamaster says not to use vinegar or organic citric-acid descalers because they react with the copper heating element and leave a lasting bad taste. For those machines, use only what the maker sells or names.

If you own a $20 Mr. Coffee-style drip pot and the manual allows vinegar, vinegar is fine. If you own a $600 espresso machine, buy the packet.

## How to descale a basic drip coffee maker

Use this only if the booklet allows vinegar or does not name a competing product.

1. Empty grounds and the filter basket. Wash the carafe.
2. Mix the acid. If the booklet gives a ratio, use it. If it does not, **equal parts distilled white vinegar and water**, or the Cuisinart-style **1/3 vinegar, 2/3 water** for a full tank, is the usual drip-machine range.
3. Pour the mix into the reservoir. Put the empty carafe in place.
4. Start a brew. Halfway through, turn the machine off and let it sit 15–30 minutes so the acid can work on the heater, not just rush through.
5. Finish the brew cycle. Dump the carafe down the sink.
6. Fill with fresh water and run a complete brew. Repeat until you cannot smell vinegar. Two cycles is a minimum; three is common.
7. Wash the carafe and lid with dish soap. Vinegar odor lives in plastic lids.

If the machine has a CLEAN button, use that sequence instead of a half-and-hold brew. The button exists because the heater needs a slower pass.

Do not descale with coffee in the basket. You will brew acid into the grounds and still leave scale in the tank.

## How to descale a single-serve pod machine

Keurig and similar machines usually have a published descale routine: empty the tank, add the named solution or a vinegar mix if that model still allows it, run brew cycles without a pod into a mug, rest, then run several water-only rinses.

Current Keurig guidance has moved many models toward their own descaling solution. If your booklet or on-machine menu says “use Keurig descaling solution,” do that. Older vinegar instructions, where they still exist, are strict about rinse count — often a cleansing brew plus several more water brews — because residual vinegar tastes like a ruined breakfast.

Never leave a pod in the holder. Never store the machine with descaler sitting in the tank overnight unless the instructions say to pause mid-cycle.

## How to descale an espresso machine

Use the manufacturer powder, tablet, or bottle. Run the official descale mode if the machine has one.

The acid has to touch every path scale can hide: the brew group, and the steam wand if you have one. That usually means pulling solution through the group, sitting for the time the booklet gives, then repeating through the wand. Rinse with full tanks of clean water until the water tastes like water.

Do not invent a vinegar cycle for a machine that ships a sachet. Aluminum boilers in particular do not love long vinegar soaks.

## After you descale

- Taste the next cup black. If it is sour, you under-rinsed. Run more water.
- Reset the descale light if the machine does not do it automatically. The booklet says how. Holding a button for three seconds is a common pattern; do not guess through every button.
- If you use a charcoal water filter in the tank, put a fresh one in after the acid rinse, not before. You do not want to soak a new filter in descaler.
- If flakes continue after a correct descale and several rinses, the tank or a line may have loose scale that needs a second cycle. If brew temperature never recovers, the heating element may already be insulated past the point a home descale can save.

## How not to do this

Do not pour CLR or a bathroom limescale gel into a coffee maker. Those are not food-path descalers. Do not mix descalers. Do not skip the rinse to save time.

Hard water is why you are descaling. If you brew every day on hard water, put descaling on a calendar instead of waiting for the light. Filtered water that still sits in the mineral range your booklet allows will stretch the interval. Distilled water is not automatically better; some espresso machines want a little mineral content for taste and sensors.
