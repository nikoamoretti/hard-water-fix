The white haze on a glass shower door is usually mineral scale, not dirt. Hard water carries dissolved calcium and magnesium. When a droplet dries on glass, those minerals stay behind as calcium carbonate and related salts. Soap and body oils bind to the same spots, so what you see is often scale plus a thin layer of soap scum.

Acids dissolve that mineral bond. Alkaline dish soap does not. That is why a glass cleaner that smells like ammonia can leave the door looking just as cloudy as it started.

## Check whether the glass is stained or etched

Before you buy anything, do a 30-second test.

Wet a cloth with distilled white vinegar and rub one stained patch for 20 to 30 seconds. Rinse and dry that patch with a microfiber cloth.

- If the patch clears, you have mineral film. The rest of this article applies.
- If the patch stays cloudy and the glass feels smooth, you may have etching. Minerals sat on the glass long enough to pit the surface. Acid will not polish pits out. More scrubbing will not either.
- If the patch stays cloudy and the glass feels rough or chalky, you still have heavy scale. Move to a commercial calcium-lime-rust cleaner before you assume the glass is ruined.

Etched glass looks milky even when it is dry and clean. Stained glass looks worse when it is wet and improves after a proper acid clean and a dry wipe.

## What you need

- Distilled white vinegar (5% acetic acid is the grocery-store version)
- A spray bottle
- Paper towels or a few clean cloths you do not mind soaking
- A non-scratch sponge
- Microfiber cloths
- A shower squeegee
- Household rubber gloves
- Optional: CLR Calcium, Lime & Rust Remover or another labeled hard-water remover
- Optional: baking soda for a paste on the bottom track only

Do not use steel wool, a scouring powder made for ovens, or a drill-mounted stiff brush on tempered shower glass. You can scratch the surface, and scratches hold the next layer of scale.

Do not mix vinegar or CLR with bleach, with an ammonia glass cleaner, or with another bathroom product “to make it stronger.” Mixing acids with bleach can release chlorine gas.

## Method 1: Vinegar for light and moderate stains

This is the right first method if the door is cloudy but you can still see through it, or if the worst buildup is a ring near the bottom.

1. Ventilate the bathroom. Turn on the fan.
2. Warm the vinegar. A minute in a microwave-safe cup is enough. Warm vinegar stays on vertical glass a little longer and works faster. Do not boil it.
3. Spray the glass until it is wet, or soak paper towels in vinegar and press them onto the stained areas. Paper towels matter on a vertical door. A quick spray runs to the floor and you have wasted the dwell time.
4. Leave the glass wet for 10 to 20 minutes. If a towel starts to dry, spray it again. Dried acid just redeposits minerals.
5. Wipe with a microfiber cloth or a non-scratch sponge. Work in small sections, top to bottom.
6. Rinse with warm water until the vinegar smell is faint.
7. Dry the glass completely. Squeegee first, then a dry microfiber pass. Leaving it to air-dry puts a new set of spots on the door you just cleaned.

For the bottom edge and the metal track, pour a little vinegar in the channel, wait 10 minutes, and lift the softened grit out with an old toothbrush. Rinse the track. Standing water in that channel is why the lower third of a door always looks worse than the top.

Vinegar is the method most household guides start with because acetic acid is mild, cheap, and effective on calcium and magnesium — the main components of typical hard-water scale. It is a weak acid. Heavy, years-old crust often needs a second pass or a stronger product.

## Method 2: CLR when vinegar stalls

If the door is still streaked after two vinegar soaks, switch products. You are not failing the method. The scale is thicker than grocery vinegar wants to chew through.

CLR Calcium, Lime & Rust Remover is a chelating acid cleaner sold specifically for calcium, lime, and rust on household glass, chrome, and similar surfaces. The household label is the one that matters, not a blog recipe.

Manufacturer directions for the household formula are stricter than vinegar:

1. Work in a well-ventilated room. Wear gloves.
2. Test a hidden corner of the glass and any coated decorative panel first.
3. Mix equal parts CLR and warm water for a standard pass. The label allows a stronger mix on heavy soil, but the dwell time does not get longer.
4. Apply with a cloth, sponge, or brush. Keep it on the glass **no more than two minutes**.
5. Rinse promptly and thoroughly with cold water.
6. Repeat the short cycle if the stain is still there. Do not “let it sit while you answer a text.”
7. Dry with a microfiber cloth.

Two minutes is the number on the bottle. Leaving a strong mineral remover on glass or metal longer than the label allows is how you get dull hardware and, on some finishes, damage.

CLR Brilliant Bath is a related bathroom spray aimed at soap scum plus mineral film. If that is what you have, the brand’s shower-door steps are: apply, wait a couple of minutes, gentle non-scratch scrub, warm rinse, dry. Read the bottle in your hand. Formulas are not interchangeable.

## What does not fix the door

**WD-40 or furniture polish as a “shine.”** Oil fills pits and the glass looks clearer for a day. The minerals are still there. The next shower’s soap sticks to the oil.

**Baking soda alone.** It is a mild abrasive and a base. It does not dissolve calcium carbonate the way an acid does. A paste can help lift softened grit from a track. It is not a substitute for vinegar or CLR on the glass.

**Bleach.** It is for organic stains and mold, not limescale. Mixed with an acid cleaner, it is a safety problem.

## Dry the glass or you will redo this next week

Every shower that air-dries on the door deposits a new mineral ring. Squeegee each pane before you leave the stall. Keep a microfiber cloth for the corners. Run the fan for 20 minutes.

A weekly maintenance wipe is a 1:1 vinegar-and-water spray, a short wait, a rinse, and a dry. That is faster than a monthly excavation.

## Slow the minerals at the tap

A shower-head filter will not soften a whole house. It can reduce some of the minerals hitting the glass. Treat it as prevention, not as a cleaner, and replace cartridges on schedule.

A whole-house softener is the actual hardness fix. It is a plumbing project. Do not expect a bottle of vinegar to change the water chemistry.

## When to stop

Stop the chemical cycle if the glass is smooth, dry, and still uniformly milky after two correct acid treatments, or if an “easy-clean” factory coating is peeling. Restoration shops polish glass with cerium oxide. For a standard builder door, replacement glass is often cheaper.

## A realistic order of operations

1. Vinegar soak, rinse, dry. Judge the result on dry glass in daylight.
2. Repeat once if you made progress.
3. Switch to a labeled calcium-lime-rust remover with a two-minute dwell.
4. Rinse cold, dry, then start the squeegee habit the same day.
5. If the door is still milky and smooth, you are looking at etch, not stain.

That sequence is slower to read than “use this miracle spray,” and it is the one that matches how the chemistry actually works.
